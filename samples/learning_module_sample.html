Round Robin Scheduling

The “fair share” scheduler: everyone gets a turn, nobody waits forever. Learn it in 12 minutes, then test yourself with GATE-level questions.

⏱ 12 min read🧪 1 simulator✍ 4 practice questions🎯 GATE: very frequent
1

The idea in one line

💡
Key ideaGive every process a fixed slice of CPU time called the time quantum. When the slice ends and the process isn't finished, send it to the back of the queue and let the next one run.
🛝
Think of a playground swing.

Four kids want the swing. Rule: each kid swings for 3 minutes, then must hop off and join the back of the line — even if they want more. Everyone keeps getting turns, no kid waits forever, and nobody can hog the swing. The swing is the CPU, the kids are processes, and “3 minutes” is the time quantum.

2

How it works, step by step

Round Robin (RR) is pre-emptive: the OS uses a timer interrupt to take the CPU away when the quantum expires.

  1. Keep a ready queue (first-in, first-out) of processes that are waiting for the CPU.
  2. Pick the process at the front and run it for at most q time units.
  3. If it finishes early (burst ≤ q), it leaves and the CPU immediately picks the next process.
  4. If the quantum expires first, the OS pre-empts it, saves its state (a context switch) and puts it at the back of the queue.
  5. Repeat until every process has finished.
⚠️
Tie-break rule used in GATEIf a new process arrives at the same instant a running process is pre-empted, the new arrival goes into the queue first, then the pre-empted process. (Unless the question says otherwise.)

4

Formulas you must know

TAT = CT − ATTurnaround
WT = TAT − BTWaiting
RT = first CPU − ATResponse
CT = completion time · AT = arrival time · BT = burst time

Waiting time is the total time spent sitting in the ready queue, not just before the first run.

CPU efficiency with switch cost s
η = q / (q + s)when processes always use full slices
How to choose the quantum?
  • Too small → the CPU spends its time switching (overhead), throughput drops.
  • Too large → behaves like FCFS, response time suffers.
  • Rule of thumb: about 80% of CPU bursts should be shorter than q.

Common mistakes (GATE traps)

🪤
Wrong queue order on tiesForgetting that a newly arrived process is queued before the pre-empted one.
🪤
Counting context switchesThe first dispatch and the final completion usually do not count. A process continuing alone is not a switch.
🪤
Waiting vs response timeResponse time stops at the first CPU access; waiting time includes all time in the ready queue.
🪤
Assuming RR minimises waiting timeSJF is optimal for average waiting time. RR optimises fairness and response.

Summary — cheat sheet

  • RR = FCFS + time quantum + pre-emption.
  • Pre-empted process → back of the ready queue; same-instant arrivals queue first.
  • Large q ⇒ FCFS · tiny q ⇒ processor sharing but heavy switching.
  • No starvation; good response time; average waiting time is usually higher than SJF.
  • TAT = CT − AT · WT = TAT − BT · RT = first run − AT.
